Section 1

Our Position

Where Architecture Is Going

The craft of architectural visualization has changed. Images that once took a specialist days can now be generated by anyone in seconds — and they are, often before an architect is ever consulted. What has not changed is what our clients require of us.

We sell inspired ideas, anchored in context and understanding, grounded in an understanding of how spaces are built and how people use them. Artificial intelligence is fast becoming part of how we develop and communicate those ideas — but an image is only as valuable as the expertise behind it. A generated picture is easy; keeping it honest is not. Outsiders don’t have the project context or the understanding to keep their images anchored to the specifics of a real project. We do — and that is the advantage this guide protects.

Visualization at VGA is not an afterthought. Every project we deliver deserves imagery that sells the idea, clarifies the complexity, or shows the design at its best.

The goal is not simply to make more images. The goal is to give clients and collaborators more ways to understand a project and more opportunities to explore ideas before decisions are made.

This document sets the firm’s expectations for that work: the visualization types we produce, the tools we use to make them, and the standard every image must meet before it leaves the office.

It is a reference for everyone in the firm — whether you’re producing your first visualization or your fiftieth — and the guide to building the skills behind it. When the firm needs visualization built out for a project, these are the skills we draw on.

These visualizations serve many audiences — clients, contractors, reviewers — and ourselves, testing ideas before decisions are made.

Our Standards

Geometry first. Every visualization starts from the actual design. The image serves the project, never the other way around.
Honest images. Concept imagery is labeled as concept. We never present an AI visualization as a photograph of something built.
Consistent styles. Our work should be recognizable as ours (see Section 4).
Verified before shared. Check every image against the model and the drawings before it leaves the office.
Human judgment first. AI proposes, transforms and assists; the architect decides.
Context matters. Site, materials, program and project constraints remain part of the source.

Section 2

AI Visualization in Practice

Four ways we use AI in our architectural process

AI is an extension of our architectural process, not a replacement for it. We use AI to help us see, test, explain and communicate ideas. It can help transform technical information into something more intuitive, generate alternatives, identify possibilities and make our designs easier for clients and team members to understand.

Same goal. More possibilities. Better understanding.

AI does not start the architecture. AI helps us explore the architecture.

The designer remains responsible for the design, technical information, accuracy and final decisions.

Understand

AI helps us interpret existing information and make it easier to digest.

Examples

  • Explain a floor plan in plain language
  • Simplify a section to 3D
  • Explain how a building is assembled
  • Highlight accessibility considerations
  • Explain complicated details to junior staff
  • Explain consultant documents simply
  • Add visual callouts to drawings
Explore

AI helps us generate possibilities and test more ideas, earlier.

Examples

  • Test 5 different facade directions
  • Explore material combinations
  • Try different roof forms
  • Explore landscape approaches
  • Develop early massing options
  • Test interior atmosphere
  • Generate precedent directions
  • Give shape to a vague client idea
Communicate

AI helps us turn technical information into visual information clients and teams can easily understand.

Examples

  • Floor plan → 3D view
  • Plan → furnished plan
  • Plan → axonometric
  • Plan → sectional perspective
  • Material study
  • Circulation diagram
  • Lighting or atmosphere concept
  • Simplified visual for non-architects
Accelerate

AI helps us handle the repetitive work, so we can spend more time on design, problem solving and people.

Examples

  • Generate and refine prompts
  • Image editing and cleanup
  • Background/context generation
  • Material studies
  • Presentation graphics
  • Diagram development
  • Concept variations
  • Initial precedent research
  • Meeting summaries

AI Visualization Rules of the Firm

AI is allowed to

  • Explore
  • Visualize
  • Explain
  • Simplify
  • Generate alternatives
  • Assist with presentations
  • Edit imagery
  • Develop diagrams
  • Help communicate design intent

AI is never the source of truth for

  • Dimensions
  • Building code requirements
  • Structural design
  • Consultant information
  • Site conditions
  • Construction details
  • Specifications
  • Existing conditions
  • Final architectural decisions

Every AI-generated image that represents a real project must be checked against the source model/drawings before it is shared as project information. If AI has invented, removed or changed something important, correct it or clearly identify the image as a concept.

Section 3

The Client Experience

What our images do for clients

For our clients, visualizations turn drawings into experiences. A plan shows what will be built; a visualization shows what it will be like to be there. These are the expectations for any image a client will see.

From plans to experiences.

Drawings show what will be built. Visualizations show what it will be like to be there.

Communicate with intent.

Match the visualization to the question being asked.

One design, multiple explanations.

The same design may need different views for different people — but the design must stay consistent across every version.

The client-facing test.

If a client can describe the design back to you from the image, they understand it. If they can’t, the visualization isn’t finished.

Disclose the AI.

Every image a client sees carries two facts: AI helped make it, and the real sources it’s based on. Titleblock title, footnote, or caption — whichever fits the deliverable, but never skip it. The firm’s line, made visible: inspired ideas, anchored in context and understanding.

  • Title: “Courtyard Concept — AI-assisted visualization”
  • Footnote: “Generated with AI assistance from the Revit model (October 2026).”
  • Early concept: “Concept study — AI-generated, not construction information.”

Our commitment.

We don’t show clients concepts we can’t build. Every visualization we share traces back to real project information — drawings, models, or verified decisions.

Section 4

The Visualization Workflow

The pipeline and the programs

The pipeline

Every visualization we make flows the same direction:

Real source inDrawings, models, site photos, precedent.
Context layeringAdd materials, entourage, site and missing context.
RenderUse Enscape + Veras to create the base image.
Render editingRefine style, details and atmosphere with AI tools.
DeliveryFinal image, checked and verified.

One rule governs the whole pipeline: nothing starts from a blank prompt.

Learn the tools in that order and everything else gets easier.

The toolbox

These are the programs the firm uses today, in pipeline order — the tools that turn a real source into a verified image.

Revit — the source of truth

You don’t need to be a power modeler for this role — but 3D visualization starts from real geometry, and Revit is where that geometry lives at VGA.

What matters for viz: setting up 3D geometry worth rendering, assigning materials (even roughly — AI can develop them), lighting settings, and exporting clean views to feed the AI tools. A well-set-up Revit view does half the visualization’s work before any rendering starts.

AutoCAD — linework as raw material

Plans, elevations, sections, and details — drawn or even exported from Revit — become inputs: clean linework underlays for AI visualization, and the base for diagrammatic treatments where the drawing itself is the star. Different traditional AutoCAD linework can even be extracted by AI to create visualization, or used to help AI set a scene.

What matters for viz: clean, legible exports with consistent lineweights and no stray geometry. AI tools read linework the way clients do — messy in, messy out.

Enscape — real-time rendering

The fastest path from a Revit model to a good-looking image: walk through live, dial in materials, drop in entourage from the asset library, save views, AI generated materials.

Two honest warnings: the material editor and the asset library are where the quality lives, and they’re also where the time goes — budget real learning time here. Learn materials first, assets second; a great material with no entourage beats a parking lot full of trees on gray boxes. AI can help add assets to a visualization.

Tip: build a small personal kit of go-to materials and assets as you learn — it becomes your speed advantage.

Veras — AI visualization inside the model

Veras takes an Enscape view and re-renders it with AI, guided by your prompt but locked to your geometry — the building stays the building. This is the sketch-to-watercolor move: massing model in, hand-painted concept out, same design.

What matters: the prompt–geometry balance. Keep geometry influence strong while learning; loosen it only when you know what you’re trading away. Prompting is the key to using AI successfully for visualization. And it may be the most difficult part to learn.

AI chatbots — visualization engines

Approved as visualization engines, not just helpers. Veras is built to work from a model; chatbots start from anything — stylised elevations, diagrams, photo edits, material studies — whole workflows outside the Revit–Enscape–Veras path.

What matters: match the engine to the source. Model in hand, Veras leads; drawing, photo, or idea in hand, the chatbot may be the whole workflow.

Bring your own tools.

This list is the starting lineup, not the whole league. If you find a tool that serves the pipeline — geometry-true, style-consistent — bring it to a check-in and make the case. Discoveries like that belong in this guide.

Section 5

The Recipes

A starting set of examples — not the complete list. New recipes get added as the work demands. Each one names its source, its path to the look, and the traps.

Recipe 1 — Early concept renders

Not realism. Raw, simple, hand-drawn feeling — visually interesting without looking finished.

  • Start with whatever exists: an early elevation sketch, AutoCAD linework, or a simple massing model. Tidy it just enough to read — this is not the time for detail.
  • Feed it to Veras or an AI chatbot with a hand-drawn style prompt: pencil and drafting-pen lines, ruler-straight edges, a light wash of colour.
  • Add simple site context: vegetation outlines, a touch of colour or shading — enough to suggest place, not enough to distract. Context can be sketched as markups on the generated output, or drawn directly on the source before you feed it in.
  • For a more developed pass, run an elevation drawing through the same path for an artistic visualisation of the elevation.
→
Trap: over-developing. A concept should look like a concept — the moment it starts looking finished, you’ve gone too far for this stage.

Recipe 2 — Linework to artistic

  • Start with linework: a Revit or AutoCAD elevation or section, or a hand sketch.
  • Feed it to Veras (or a chatbot image edit) with an artistic prompt — watercolor, hand-painted — keeping the drawing’s proportions locked.
  • Develop in passes: first the architectural lines read as drawn; then wash, tone, and simple context build the atmosphere.
→
Trap: losing the drawing. If the AI redraws the proportions, tighten geometry control — the art serves the linework, not the other way around.

Recipe 3 — Photoreal

  • In Revit: set up the project geometry, place Enscape assets (their locations live in the model), and adjust lighting. Use Enscape to generate realistic materials — a chatbot can help isolate and produce a desired material for Enscape to use.
  • In Enscape: walk around to find the view. Set the camera position, dial in the mood and lighting, refine material properties, set the quality of the source image.
  • Import the image into Veras: this is where prompting begins and the visualization comes to life. Direct the style and atmosphere, and use Veras’s editing tools to refine details toward the final image.
→
Trap: skipping the composition. No prompt saves a bad camera angle — find the view in Enscape before anything else.

Recipe 4 — Linework to diagrammatic

  • Start with linework from Revit, AutoCAD, or a hand-drawn sketch — whatever communicates the idea fastest.
  • Decide the story first: one story per image.
  • Stylize with Veras or a chatbot toward a diagrammatic look.
→
Trap: two stories in one diagram. If it needs a paragraph of explanation, split it.

Recipe 5 — Detail to isometric

For: complex millwork, tricky geometry, anything a flat detail can’t explain.

  • In Revit, isolate the detail in a 3D axonometric view — hide everything that isn’t the story.
  • Render the base in Enscape (clean materials, neutral background) or push it through Veras for a softer illustrative feel.
  • Keep annotations light: a few leaders, no paragraphs. The image does the explaining.
→
Trap: showing the whole building. An isometric of everything explains nothing — crop ruthlessly to the detail.

Recipe 6 — Material Exploration

  • Start from a controlled project view — fixed camera, locked geometry.
  • Test a small number of material directions (three to five, not thirty), keeping geometry and openings identical across every study.
  • Present the studies side by side as a material conversation, not a beauty contest.
→
Trap: letting the study replace the spec. These images explore direction; the specification still decides.

Recipe 7 — Existing to Proposed

  • Start with a real photograph or a verified existing-conditions model — the more accurate, the better.
  • Overlay or transform toward the proposed design, keeping the existing context recognizable: same viewpoint, same neighbors, same street.
  • Check everything the AI touched against the source — especially the parts that were supposed to stay the same.
→
Trap: letting the AI quietly redesign the existing. If the neighboring building changed, the image is wrong.

Recipe 8 — Concept Presentation

  • Select only studies that answer a question — one question per image.
  • Sequence them as a visual story: existing → exploration → direction. Order matters more than quantity.
  • Check the story against the project narrative — if an image doesn’t serve a decision, cut it.
→
Trap: filling the board with attractive images that don’t communicate a design decision. Pretty is not a purpose.

The pattern underneath

Every recipe is the same move: real source in → controlled transformation → verified image out. When you’re unsure which recipe fits, ask: what is this image supposed to make someone understand? The answer picks the recipe.

AI Chatbots

AI chatbots are a legitimate source of rendering capability. Section 7

Prompt Engineering for Visualization

The language of intent

What prompting is. A prompt is the language AI uses to understand intent — what you want it to do. AI engines are not perfect machines; they read language about as naturally as humans do, which means they misinterpret intent the way any human would. Expect that. Multiple attempts to get close are normal. Iteration is the key skill, not a sign of failure.

Prompting for visualization happens in two stages. Stage one sets the style and creates the base visualization. Stage two takes that base image as the new source and uses prompting — plus markups and reference images — to edit and fine-tune it into the final visualization.

Stage one — Set the style, create the base

Chaos recommends building the prompt in order, one ingredient at a time:

TaskWhat the AI should do.
StyleThe look to apply.
ComponentsWhat stays, what’s added.
CameraViewpoint and framing.
LightingMood, sun, atmosphere.
ConstraintsWhat must not change.
Generated imageThe base visualization.

The trick: write each ingredient as bullet-point directions, not a paragraph. Bullets are clearer and more to the point — easier for the AI to parse for intent and priorities.

The following is an example prompt for a photorealistic exterior, worked in order:

Task

  • Enhance and embellish the provided building design into a photorealistic architectural masterpiece.

Style & Art Direction

  • High-end architectural photography.
  • “ArchDaily” editorial style.
  • 4K high-resolution.
  • Ultra-detailed.

Components

  • Main Subject: a modern building.
  • Setting: situated in a modern city on an overcast day — soft shadows, dramatic sky.
  • Details: add physical material micro-details like visible wood grain, concrete pores, and realistic reflections on glass surfaces.

Lighting

  • Lighting: volumetric sun rays filtering through trees; warm interior ambient lighting visible through windows to create depth and “life”.
  • Color: balance the photo with warm and cold color tone mixtures and good contrast.

Constraints

  • Maintain the exact structural silhouette of the original design.
  • No over-smoothing or “plastic” AI look; prioritize raw, unretouched texture fidelity.
  • Ensure all structural lines are perfectly vertical and parallel (no lens distortion).

The prompting video in Section 6’s chatbot shelf walks through similar methods — same ingredients, different words.

Stage two — Edit and fine-tune

Stage two starts from the latest iteration. Clear the prompt and write a new one — this time the job is not creating the image but changing it. Four ways to direct the edits:

  1. Describe it in words. Write a new prompt describing what you want changed. Trap: the AI may change more than you asked for — a fresh prompt is a blunt instrument, and unmentioned elements can drift.
  2. Markup the image. Markup the image to label or detail the edits, then prompt the next iteration to focus on the markups while holding the remainder of the image. Take care: focused prompting still needs precision to avoid unintentional edits.
  3. Add a reference image. Drop a reference into the chat or the Veras editor — a material sample, a style reference — then prompt the AI to apply it to the desired elements.
  4. Combine them. Markups, reference images, and prompts together, each doing what it does best — the most controlled path to the final visualization.

Section 9

The Last Word

This guide began with a position: our advantage is the project knowledge, architectural judgment, and context behind the image. Everything since — the pipeline, the recipes, the training shelf, the prompting method — is technique in service of that position. Technique changes. The position doesn’t.

Keep the dialogue open.

This playbook only works as an open dialogue. AI is not a static world — new tools, new workflows, new tricks will keep arriving, and this guide has to flow with those changes. When you find a valuable workflow or a useful piece of software, bring it to the team. We’ll write the worthwhile ones into the next edition.

The standard doesn’t move.

Every image traces to a real source. Every client image is checked against that source, discloses how AI was used, and goes out deliberately. That discipline doesn’t move.

Judgment is the job.

AI proposes, transforms, and assists; the architect decides. A beautiful image of the wrong building is a failure.

Make images that help people understand the architecture.

Everything else is technique.
